I am happy to supervise MSc summer projects in the field of empirical macroeconomics and econometrics. Below I outline four themes. The first two focus on structural shock identification in macroeconomics, while the third and fourth centre on macroeconomic reduced-form modelling and forecasting. These topics all require a basic understanding of econometrics as well as some experience with statistical software (e.g., R).

Format of the summer project. I am happy to supervise both options. Option A, the academic-style dissertation (up to 10,000 words), is the classic MSc thesis and likely the better choice if you want to write an academic piece and aim for a research position after our MSc (e.g., a PhD). But I also encourage you to do Option B, in particular if you aim for a position in industry or at a policy institution, and especially now that AI tools are widely available. Here the written report is shorter (5,000–6,000 words), but it includes two additional pieces of assessment for a non-technical audience (e.g., slides, a policy brief, a poster, or a video). Be creative and innovative! For example, we have not studied Shiny apps in R, but you could ask AI to implement the technicalities for you. That is, once you have ensured that your code (e.g., a VAR) runs, let AI set up a Shiny app for you. In this case, carefully document your workflow and prompts as a skill or Markdown (.md) file.

Structural shock identification: readings and data

External instruments are increasingly used to first identify a specific structural macroeconomic shock and then assess how this shock propagates through the macroeconomy. The following readings provide a broad overview of structural shock identification in macroeconomics:

  • Ramey, V. A. (2016). Macroeconomic shocks and their propagation. Handbook of Macroeconomics, 2, 71–162. [DOI]
  • Nakamura, E. & Steinsson, J. (2018). Identification in macroeconomics. Journal of Economic Perspectives, 32(3), 59–86. [DOI]

Available shock series and instruments. A harmonised and up-to-date collection of structural macroeconomic shocks (for use as instruments) is a good starting point.

Monetary policy:

  • Altavilla, C., Brugnolini, L., Gürkaynak, R. S., Motto, R., & Ragusa, G. (2019). Measuring euro area monetary policy. Journal of Monetary Economics, 108, 162–179. [DOI] [EA-MPD database]
  • Braun, R., Miranda-Agrippino, S., & Saha, T. (2025). Measuring monetary policy in the UK: The UK monetary policy event-study database. Journal of Monetary Economics, 149, 103645. [DOI] [UK-MPD database]
  • Jarociński, M. (2024). Estimating the Fed’s unconventional policy shocks. Journal of Monetary Economics, 144, 103548. [DOI] [MP shocks]

Energy:

  • Känzig, D. R. (2021). The macroeconomic effects of oil supply news: Evidence from OPEC announcements. American Economic Review, 111(4), 1092–1125. [DOI] [Oil supply surprises and news shocks]
  • Alessandri, P. & Gazzani, A. (2025). Natural gas and the macroeconomy: Not all energy shocks are alike. Journal of Monetary Economics, 151, 103749. [DOI] [Gas supply shocks]

Climate policy:

  • Känzig, D. R. (2026). The unequal economic consequences of carbon pricing. American Economic Review, 116(11), 4003–4040. [Paper] [Carbon policy surprises and shocks]

Weather and climate:

  • Dell, M., Jones, B. F., & Olken, B. A. (2012). Temperature shocks and economic growth: Evidence from the last half century. American Economic Journal: Macroeconomics, 4(3), 66–95. [DOI] [Data]
  • Ciccarelli, M., Kuik, F., & Martínez Hernández, C. (2024). The asymmetric effects of temperature shocks on inflation in the largest euro area countries. European Economic Review, 168, 104805. [DOI] [Climate data]
  • Kim, H. S., Matthes, C., & Phan, T. (2025). Severe weather and the macroeconomy. American Economic Journal: Macroeconomics, 17(2), 315–341. [DOI] [Climate data]
